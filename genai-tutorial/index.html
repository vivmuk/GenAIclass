How machines learned, and the market they became.

A field guide from Ada Lovelace's Note G to the price of a million tokens in 2026.

By Vivek Mukhatyar.

Set in markup by claude-opus-5-5, 16 November 2026.

1843–1958. The idea that a machine could be taught.

  1. 1843

    Ada Lovelace, Note G

    Her notes on Babbage's Analytical Engine set out a program for a machine not yet built.

  2. 1943

    McCulloch and Pitts

    An artificial neuron: a unit that fires when its weighted inputs cross a threshold.

  3. 1950

    Turing asks the question

    Computing Machinery and Intelligence asks whether machines can think, and how we would tell.

  4. 1956

    Dartmouth names the field

    A summer workshop at Dartmouth College gives the work its name: artificial intelligence.

  5. 1958

    Rosenblatt's perceptron

    The Mark I wires 400 photocells to weights, and the weights learn from mistakes. The plate in the margin runs his rule.

1966–1974. The first promise, the first bill.

  1. 1966

    ELIZA and the DOCTOR script

    Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA matched patterns in typed sentences, and its DOCTOR script answered like a therapist. It understood nothing; its users credited it with understanding.

  2. 1969

    Perceptrons

    Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert proved what a single layer of weights cannot compute. XOR, one input or the other but not both, is among them.

  3. 1973

    The Lighthill report

    James Lighthill told Britain's Science Research Council the field had not delivered. British funding collapsed, and the first winter began.

You have been training the plate in the margin since the title page; asked for XOR, it will not settle.

Sources: Weizenbaum 1966; Minsky and Papert 1969; Lighthill 1973.

1980–1993. Rules bought, rules broken.

Industry bought intelligence written down by hand, one rule at a time. Learning carried on out of fashion, and out of the money.

1980

XCON

At Digital Equipment Corporation, the expert system XCON configures computer orders from a hand-built rule base.

1982

Fifth Generation

Japan's Fifth Generation Computer project sets out to build machines that reason in logic. Rules are the national bet.

1986

Backpropagation

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularise it: send the error backward through the layers and adjust every weight.

The second winter

The rule bases proved costly to keep, and the money left again. Backpropagation was kept alive through the cold. In the margin, the plate has a second layer now, and XOR is solved.

Sources: McDermott 1980; Moto-oka 1982; Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams 1986.

1997–2017. The decade the curve bent.

  1. 1997

    Deep Blue

    IBM's chess machine beats the world champion, Garry Kasparov, in a match.

  2. 2009

    ImageNet

    A labelled image collection large enough to train, and to judge, vision at scale.

  3. 2012

    AlexNet

    60 million parameters, 15.3 percent top-5 error.

  4. 2016

    AlphaGo

    DeepMind's program beats Lee Sedol at Go.

  5. 2017

    Attention Is All You Need

    The transformer: attention without recurrence.

Sources: Campbell et al. 2002; Deng et al. 2009; Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton 2012; Silver et al. 2016; Vaswani et al. 2017.

Why attention scaled.

A recurrent network reads a sentence one word at a time, and each step waits for the one before it, so training cannot spread one sequence across parallel hardware.

Attention lets every position look at every other position in a single step. All of those pairwise relations are computed at once as matrix arithmetic, which is exactly what accelerators do well. Make the model and the data larger, and the hardware keeps up.

Source: Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need, 2017.
